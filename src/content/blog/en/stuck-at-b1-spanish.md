At an intermediate level, you can already manage many situations, but progress may feel less visible than it did at the beginning. You learn new words and understand more, yet when you speak you return to the same expressions, hesitate over certain structures, or lose track when a conversation speeds up.

That does not necessarily mean that you have stopped improving. It may mean that you need to stop treating “Spanish” as one broad goal and find the specific skill that is limiting you now.

## First, identify where you get stuck

Before adding more exercises, spend a few days noticing what happens when you use the language. These questions can help:

- **Listening:** do you struggle with every conversation, or only with certain accents, subjects, or speeds?
- **Speaking:** do you know what you want to say but lack the words, or is the structure itself still unclear?
- **Interaction:** can you answer but find it difficult to ask questions, clear up a misunderstanding, or hold your turn?
- **Accuracy:** do you repeat mistakes that you recognise when you see them in writing?
- **Confidence:** do you avoid structures you know because you are not sure you can use them correctly?

Choose one or two real examples. “I want to speak better” is difficult to turn into focused practice; “I want to explain a problem at work without switching to English” gives you a much more useful starting point.

## Four ways to work on an intermediate-level block

### 1. Practise situations you actually need

Make a short list of conversations that are common or coming up: speaking to a neighbour, taking part in a meeting, attending a medical appointment, or dealing with an administrative task. Prepare the vocabulary and questions that are likely to appear, rehearse the situation, and then review what was missing.

You do not need to memorise a complete dialogue. The aim is to have several ways to explain an idea, ask for clarification, or gain time while you think.

### 2. Turn authentic content into active practice

A news item, interview, or video can help if its difficulty allows you to work with it. Listen once for the main idea. Then choose a short section, note useful expressions, and summarise it in your own words.

You do not have to understand everything. If the material is so difficult that you can only guess, choose something more accessible or work with a shorter excerpt. The difficulty should give you something specific to practise, not just leave you tired.

### 3. Reuse vocabulary and structures

Recognising an expression does not guarantee that you can use it in conversation. Choose a few expressions connected to your goal and use them several times: make your own examples, answer a question, tell a story, and return to them a few days later.

The same applies to grammar. Instead of reviewing a rule in isolation, place it inside a communication task. For example, if you want to practise hypotheses, compare real options; if you are working on past tenses, describe an incident and answer questions about it.

### 4. Ask for corrections you can apply

Correcting every detail while you speak can interrupt the conversation, but never receiving correction allows some problems to persist. One useful option is to choose a single priority for each session: linking words, past tenses, the pronunciation of particular sounds, or one specific structure.

Keep short examples of your mistakes and the better alternative. In your next practice session, create opportunities to use that correction. This helps you see whether it has moved from explanation into use.

## How to tell whether the plan is working

You do not need to promise yourself a date for reaching another level. Compare similar tasks and look for observable changes:

- you can explain an idea with fewer pauses;
- you understand a type of conversation that used to be difficult;
- you have more ways to clarify or rephrase;
- you spontaneously use a structure you have been practising;
- you notice and correct a recurring mistake yourself.

Record one or two examples after each practice session. If the same objective does not change after several opportunities, narrow its scope, change the exercise, or ask for help identifying the obstacle more precisely.

## A simple next step

Choose one situation that is difficult for you today and record or write a first response. Identify what prevented you from expressing yourself as you wanted. Then practise only that element and repeat a comparable task.

Moving beyond a plateau is not about doing more things at once. It is about observing more carefully, choosing a priority, and giving that skill enough opportunities to appear in real conversations.
